Furniture AI · Ken Tam2. Motivation

2. Motivation

Someone renting an unfurnished apartment has to buy a bed, a sofa and storage for rooms they may have seen once, often from a marketing floor plan with no measurements they trust.

Who has the problem

The person I designed for is a renter moving into a new apartment with a fixed amount to spend. They usually have three things: the building's published floor plan (an image on the leasing website, often a 3D marketing render), a budget, and a rough idea of a style. They usually do not have a tape measure reading of every wall, a designer, or time to keep twenty store tabs consistent.

How it is handled today

  • Tab sprawl. Each store has its own site, sizes and prices. Comparing a bed frame from one store with a sofa from another means copying numbers into a list by hand, and the list goes stale as prices change.
  • Guessing the room. A marketing render may omit dimensions or obscure boundaries; fixtures and openings still need review. Measuring before moving in is often not possible, so people guess.
  • Budget drift. It is easy to fall in love with one expensive piece and discover later that the rest of the apartment no longer fits the budget.
  • Unclear sources. General web search mixes official product pages with marketplaces, resellers and stale listings; it is hard to tell what was actually checked and when.

Why it matters

The cost is uncertainty rather than one dramatic failure: time spent re-checking, pieces that turn out too large, and money spent before the whole set is known. I have not measured these costs for renters in general, so this report makes no claim about how large they are. The aim of Furniture AI is narrower and testable: turn the plan the renter already has into one consistent, budgeted, sourced shopping list, and say plainly what is approximate.

Design principle. The app should never pretend to know more than it does. Missing facts stay missing, the plan reading is labelled as an estimate, every product carries the date its page was checked, and the budget is enforced by code rather than by the model's judgement.

What a good outcome looks like

For the renter, success is a list they can act on: every piece they asked for has a real product with a price, a size and a link; the total is inside the budget with money held back for delivery and tax; and anything uncertain (a room the plan reader mislabelled, a product larger than its draft outline) is visible rather than hidden.

3. What is new here

AI room planners already exist, and some overlap closely with parts of Furniture AI. I checked three on their own official pages on October 26, 2026. I did not run a hands-on comparison, so the table below describes what each product says it does, not a benchmark.

ProductWhat its official pages describeOverlap and difference

Strong visualisation of one room; the catalogue is IKEA's own. Furniture AI is plan-first, covers the whole apartment and mixes stores.
Planner 5DAI recognition turns an uploaded 2D floor plan into an editable 3D project (paid plans). Its documentation recommends a bird's-eye plan with dimensions and warns that renders or elevations cannot be properly recreated.Offers 3D editing. What Furniture AI combines

  1. Starts from the building, not a blank room. For a known building it lists the leasing site's published plans (19 for The Archive, filterable by bedroom count), so the renter picks a plan rather than drawing one.
  2. Explicit uncertainty and provenance. The plan reading is labelled approximate, rooms whose use is uncertain get no automatic furniture, the original image is kept unchanged, and every product shows the date its page was checked.
  3. Budget-aware choices across several retailers. One budget for the whole apartment, a share held back for delivery and tax, a planning share per piece, and a server-side refusal of anything over what is left.
  4. Editable arrangement and a portable result. Pieces can be moved, removed or added; the result leaves the app as a printable HTML checklist and an Excel file with working store links.

This is a useful combination for renters, not a claim to be the first AI room planner or the best one. Competing products do more in 3D, and Cona overlaps closely on priced products.

4. The agents

Furniture AI is a single orchestrator written in plain Python. It decides when to call a model and checks everything a model returns. There are four typed agents built with PydanticAI and one bounded web-research call, all on gpt-6-luna through the course Portkey Responses API.

Tools. The four agents have no app tools: each request carries only the structured-output schema, so a model cannot fetch a page, change a price or write to the database. The web-research call's only tool is the provider's built-in web search; it returns cited URLs, which code treats as leads.

Model choice. gpt-6-luna is the only model allowed on the course key. It can read images and return structured output, which covers what the plan, photo, preference and helper agents need. I did not benchmark it against other models. The model name is fixed in code, and tests check that requests go out with it.

One real run, step by step

The demo was recorded in a real browser on October 26, 2026, about 16:06–16:12 EDT, with a room-use review afterwards (16:23–16:25). Setup: a new place for The Archive, 848 Chapel Street, New Haven, CT 06510, used as a public example building. Settings: new items only, modern style, delivery, and a $3,000 budget with 10% held back. No photos were uploaded.

Step 1 · Find the building's plans (no AI)

The building comes from a checked list, so no search or model call is needed. The app shows the leasing site's 19 published plan types and filters them by bedroom count. It says these are plan types, not every unit variant, and that banner text is the property's marketing, not verified availability.

I chose the published plan named 848 | 1x1 A, one of the 8 one-bedroom plans. The app takes no size or area from a plan's name. The public image is stored unchanged as the original; later steps work on versioned derivatives.

Plan source: The Archive's official floor-plan page, entrata.thearchiveapts.com/new-haven/the-archive/conventional/; public plan image (accessed October 26, 2026). Plan image © its owner; shown for academic illustration.

Step 2 · Read the floor plan (Plan Map agent, 1 call)

"Read floor plan & continue" sends the image plus two framed crops of it in one vision call. The model returns a typed map; code validates the coordinates and decides each area's use from the evidence.

Result: 7 areas. The plan has no printed room dimensions, so everything is drawn at an approximate relative scale. The call used 3,775 input tokens (1,313 cached) and 2,908 output tokens, with 22.7 s of model time.

Step 3 · Arrange your space (code only), then a human review

Code draws a clean plan from the map and places six draft pieces: a queen bed frame, queen mattress, nightstand, dresser, sofa and coffee table. Kitchen, bath and closet areas are kept clear.

The model mislabelled three areas. It called the living area Dining, a closet Balcony, and a utility space a second Bath. After the first pass, I reviewed the map against the published plan and fixed all three with the app's own room-use control: Dining to Living, Balcony to Closet, second Bath to Laundry.

Each fix is saved as a new map revision, the original AI reading is kept, and all six chosen products stayed selected. This was a room-use review only. No geometry was measured or validated, the kitchen and circulation area stays one kept-clear "Hall", and fit is not verified. 3D marketing renders like this need that review.

Step 4 · Choose furniture (3 web-research calls + code)

"Find furniture" handled the six piece types. Three already had enough saved options that fit the budget, so they were not searched. The other three each got one web-research call (9 web searches in total, 3 per call). Only cited pages from approved stores were kept as leads, and code reads a page before it can become an option.

"Choose for me" then filled all six slots for $1,956.97. In the interface I swapped the mattress from the $199 ÅSVANG to the $399 VALEVÅG; every option line shows its fit estimate and the remaining budget.

Step 5 · Final checklist (code only)

The final list totals $2,156.97 for six items, all chosen, against a $2,700.00 item allowance. The six pieces are listed with their prices in Section 7 (Figures 5–6). Both downloads were saved through the browser and checked as files (see Section 7).

Per user and at scale (stated assumption)

Assume a renter runs the full flow 4 times a month while furnishing. This is an assumption, not a measurement. Multiplying the measured run cost:

ScaleMonthly estimated model + search cost
1 user (4 runs)$0.39
100 users$39.14
10,000 users$3,914.46

Formula: $0.09786147 per run × 4 runs × users. This excludes hosting, storage, support and payment fees, and uses today's rates as written in the code.

What keeps the cost down (all built and in the code)

  • Saved catalogue first. If a piece type already has 3 or more distinct saved options that fit and are affordable, no web search runs; three of the six types were skipped this way in this run.
  • One search per piece type and size. Two identical beds share one search, and repeat searches reuse a saved result for 10 minutes.
  • Shared in-flight requests. Identical searches that arrive at the same time share one request, so they are never paid twice; a failure is not cached.
  • Bounded checks. At most 12 pages per search are read, two at a time, stopping at 3 ready options. A marketplace that refuses automated reading is skipped without spending a search.
  • Nothing extra runs by itself. "Search more" is an explicit button.
  • Limits. Estimated cost is reserved before every call against a per-session allowance ($2.00), a daily allowance ($3.00) and 12 requests per 10 minutes; a limit pauses the search with a plain message.

Did cutting cost hurt quality?

There is a real risk, though I have not measured it. Relying on a small saved catalogue keeps cost near 10 cents, but it narrows the choice. All six products in this run came from two retailers, five of them from IKEA pages checked on October 24, and saved prices can be stale until rechecked. The pilot user's feedback about finding pieces she liked (Section 7) is consistent with that risk, but it does not prove the cost controls caused it.

An offline benchmark (docs/evidence/offline_search_benchmark.md) measured two concurrent page checks against one at a time, on a fake network with an equal 80 ms per page: median 0.372 s versus 0.594 s, with identical decisions. That is a simulation on a fixture, not live speed, and it says nothing about quality beyond that fixture.

Furniture AI · Ken Tam6. Safety

6. Safety

The risks that matter here are untrusted web content, the app being used to reach private networks, a model inventing facts or taking the wrong action, and money: both the user's furniture budget and the token budget.

Attacks

  • Prompt injection from pages, listings and images. Product pages are parsed by deterministic code and are never sent to a model. Text the model sees in images is evidence, not instruction. Model outputs can only fill typed schemas, which code validates: a model cannot set a price, choose a product, change the budget or call a tool.
  • Server-side request forgery. Every fetch goes through a SafeFetcher. It allows only https/http on default ports with no credentials in the URL, resolves DNS and refuses private, loopback, link-local and metadata addresses, pins the connection to the checked IP, checks every redirect hop before connecting, and caps size, type and redirects.
  • Untrustworthy sources. Product results are kept only from an allowlist: Amazon, Article, IKEA and Target for new items, and individual listings on five named resale marketplaces for used ones. The allowlist does not make a seller trustworthy: an approved marketplace does not verify sellers, condition, stock or delivery, and the app says so.
  • Key leakage. The key is read from the environment or a .env file (the repository folder, then the one above it, never higher). It is never shown, logged or sent to the browser.

Malfunction

  • Invented facts. Prices, sizes and stock come only from checked pages; missing facts stay unknown and those products are not offered. Plan readings are validated and labelled approximate.
  • Wrong actions. The design helper can only propose typed changes; nothing changes until the user ticks them and presses Apply, and they apply once, to one version. Designs are append-only versions with conflict checks.
  • Loops. One model call per request with at most one validation retry, a bounded number of page checks, and no automatic second search.

Overspending

  • The user's money. Options over a piece's share or the whole remaining budget are not recommended, and the server refuses any choice that exceeds what is left, with the exact figures. Earlier over-budget choices are flagged for replacement, never silently deleted.
  • The token budget. Estimated cost is reserved atomically before each call against session, daily and rate limits. These are estimates from a rate card, not a guarantee of the provider's invoice; the observed extra web searches (Section 5) show why the margin matters.

Regression: a $14,500 sofa on a $3,000 budget

This test rebuilds a real failure. Before it was fixed, a $14,500 used sofa appeared as a choosable recommendation on a $3,000 budget. With $2,700 available for items, the test checks four things:

  • the sofa is not recommended;
  • it is not listed even in the broader within-budget view;
  • the server refuses to choose it with HTTP 422 and the figures ($14,500.00 against $2,700.00 left);
  • the design is unchanged.

Other defenses in the run

  • Prompt injection: an injected "ignore the budget" instruction cannot change budget or policy.
  • SSRF: localhost, private ranges, the cloud metadata address, non-http schemes, credentials in URLs, DNS answers pointing to private hosts, and redirects to metadata or rebinding hosts are all refused, and the connection is pinned to the checked IP.
  • Sources: unknown sites and redirects to unknown sites are refused before any network access.
  • Configuration: the parent-folder .env is found while a grandparent is ignored, the status page never contains the key, and requests go out with gpt-6-luna only.

Furniture AI · Ken Tam8. Limits + next steps

8. Limits + next steps

What does not work well yet

  • Plan reading needs review. On the 3D marketing render in this run, the reader mislabelled three areas (living as Dining, a closet as Balcony, a utility space as Bath). A manual review in the app fixed them, but it took a person to notice. Shapes and scale are approximate, and fit is never verified, especially without printed dimensions.
  • Tiny catalogue, manual adapters. Checked products come from a small saved set built with page-specific adapters (IKEA, Article, one Amazon item). Prices go stale until rechecked, and taste variety is limited, as the pilot user found.
  • Secondhand is unreliable. In practice eBay listing pages refuse automated reading (HTTP 403), AptDeco often shows robot checks, and Chairish is readable but often above budget. The app never bypasses these: it skips a refusing marketplace and says so, which can leave little or no affordable used supply.
  • Buildings. Plan discovery works for buildings whose sites publish plans or that are in the checked list; it is not a universal apartment database. The user can always upload a plan image.
  • Local only. No public hosting and no accounts; cost caps are estimates rather than an invoice guarantee. Evidence of impact is one demo and one informal pilot.

Next month, in priority order

  1. Taste and variety. More stores and more styles per piece type, so the first recommendations match what people actually like.
  2. Approved data feeds. Replace scraped pages with retailer or marketplace feeds and APIs where allowed, so prices and stock are current and secondhand supply does not depend on pages that block automated reading.
  3. Better plan review and geometry calibration. Ask the user to confirm room uses before placing furniture, and let one known wall length calibrate the scale.
  4. Independent task timing. A small study with several renters, timing the app against their usual method, with their consent.

Longer term (not built)

Reusing a renter's existing furniture inventory between moves, and peer resale when they leave, would extend the same plan-first list.
